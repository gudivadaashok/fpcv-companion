Image Segmentation, Interactively

Split an image into regions of similar pixels. Compare k-means on color, mean shift, and graph-based segmentation, and click any pixel to see why it landed where it did.

Pick a sample or upload a photo. Everything runs on a small copy of the image in your browser. Follows the Image Segmentation lectures of First Principles of Computer Vision.

00

Choose an image

Colorful images with distinct objects work best. Large photos are shrunk to 160 px (100 px for mean shift) before segmenting.

01

K-means on color (and position)

Each pixel becomes a point in CIE Lab color space. K-means picks \(k\) centroids, then alternates two steps: assign every pixel to its nearest centroid, and move each centroid to the mean of its pixels. Press Step to watch each half-step. Click the images to inspect a pixel.

The two steps

\[ \text{assign:}\quad c_i = \arg\min_j \|f_i - \mu_j\|^2 \]
\[ \text{update:}\quad \mu_j = \operatorname{mean}\{ f_i : c_i = j \} \]
\[ \text{objective:}\quad J = \sum_i \|f_i - \mu_{c_i}\|^2 \]

The feature \(f\) is \((L, a, b)\), or \((L, a, b, \lambda x, \lambda y)\) when position weight is on. Each full iteration lowers \(J\) or leaves it unchanged, so the process stops. It may stop at a poor answer: try New random start. With \(\lambda\) above 0, nearby pixels prefer the same cluster, so regions become compact. The result depends on \(k\), and a gradient such as sky gets split in bands.

02

Mean-shift segmentation

Mean shift needs no \(k\). Each pixel climbs the density of nearby, similar pixels in a joint space of position \((x, y)\) and color \((L, a, b)\) until it stops at a peak. Pixels that reach the same peak form one region. Click a pixel to see its climb.

The mean-shift step

\[ y \leftarrow \operatorname{mean}\{ z_j : \|x_j - x_y\| \le h_s \text{ and } \|c_j - c_y\| \le h_r \} \]

Here \(z = (x, y, L, a, b)\) and the window is flat. Repeat until \(y\) stops moving. The converged \(y\) is the pixel's mode. This page merges neighboring pixels whose modes lie within \(h_s/2\) in position and \(h_r/2\) in color, then merges regions smaller than the minimum size into their most similar neighbor. Larger bandwidths give fewer, smoother regions. Small \(h_r\) keeps fine color differences apart. The image is downsampled to 100 px to keep this fast.

03

Graph-based segmentation (Felzenszwalb–Huttenlocher)

Treat pixels as graph nodes joined to their neighbors by edges weighted by color difference. Sort the edges from lightest to heaviest. Merge two regions across an edge when the edge is light compared with how varied each region already is. Slide the progress bar to replay the merging.

The merge rule

\[ \mathrm{Int}(C) = \text{largest edge inside the minimum spanning tree of } C \]
\[ \text{merge } C_1, C_2 \text{ across edge } w \iff w \le \min\!\left( \mathrm{Int}(C_1) + \frac{k}{|C_1|},\; \mathrm{Int}(C_2) + \frac{k}{|C_2|} \right) \]

A region's internal variation \(\mathrm{Int}(C)\) is the heaviest edge needed to hold it together. The term \(k/|C|\) gives small regions extra tolerance, so single pixels merge easily and large regions only merge across clearly lighter edges. A larger \(k\) favors larger regions. After the main pass, any region below the minimum size merges into a neighbor across its lightest edge. Edge weights are RGB distances between 8-connected neighbors of the smoothed image.

04

Side by side

The three methods answer the same question with different assumptions. K-means needs k and ignores layout unless you add position. Mean shift needs bandwidths. Graph-based segmentation needs a scale and respects connectivity.
