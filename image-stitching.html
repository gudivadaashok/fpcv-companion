Image Stitching, Interactively

To stitch two photos, find the same points in both, then solve for the \(3 \times 3\) homography that maps one image onto the other. RANSAC makes that solution survive wrong matches.

The default pair comes from one wide procedural scene, so the true homography is known and you can check the estimate against it. Follows the Image Stitching lecture in the Features chapter of First Principles of Computer Vision.

01

Harris corners

A good feature changes in every direction when you shift a small window. The structure tensor \(M\) sums the gradient products over a window. If both eigenvalues of \(M\) are large, the point is a corner. If only one is large, it is an edge. Click a pixel in view A or in the response map to see the numbers.

The formulas

Keep local maxima of \(R\) in a \(9 \times 9\) window above the threshold.

Corners within 16 px of the border or outside the scene are skipped so that each one has a full patch around it.

02

Patch descriptors and ratio-test matching

Each corner gets a descriptor: an \(8 \times 8\) grid of samples spaced 4 px apart (a 32 px window) from a blurred copy of the image. Subtracting the mean and dividing by the spread removes brightness and contrast changes. Each corner in A is matched to its nearest descriptor in B, and kept only if the nearest distance is clearly smaller than the second nearest. Click a corner in A to inspect it.

03

RANSAC with a normalized DLT

Some matches are wrong, and a least-squares fit to all of them would be ruined. RANSAC repeats: pick 4 matches at random, solve for \(H\) exactly, and count how many other matches agree within a pixel threshold. The model with the most inliers wins and is refit on all its inliers. Press Step to watch it work.

Normalized DLT for one sample

1. Shift and scale each point set so its centroid is 0 and its mean distance is \(\sqrt{2}\) (matrices \(T\), \(T'\)).

2. Each match \((x,y) \to (u,v)\) gives two rows of \(A\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} -x & -y & -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & ux & uy & u \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -x & -y & -1 & vx & vy & v \end{bmatrix} \]

3. Find \(\mathbf{h}\) with \(\|A\mathbf{h}\|\) minimal and \(\|\mathbf{h}\| = 1\). That is the eigenvector of \(A^\top A\) with the smallest eigenvalue (this page uses a Jacobi eigen solver).

4. Undo the normalization: \(H = T'^{-1}\tilde H\, T\).

Skipping step 1 makes the numbers in \(A\) span from 1 to 100,000 and the solution becomes unstable.

Warp, blend, and compare with the truth
