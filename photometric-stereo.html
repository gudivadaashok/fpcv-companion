Photometric Stereo, Interactively

Photograph a surface three times, each time lit from a different direction. From brightness alone, recover the direction of every surface point, its albedo, and then its 3D shape.

The surfaces below are Lambertian (matte) and rendered in your browser, so the true answer is known and you can measure the error. Follows the Radiometry and Reflectance and Photometric Stereo lectures.

02

Solve each pixel

Stack the three light directions as rows of a matrix \(\mathbf{S}\). At each pixel, the three brightness values give \(\rho\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{I}\). The length of that vector is the albedo \(\rho\) and its direction is the normal \(\mathbf{n}\).

The equations

\[ \begin{bmatrix} I_1\\ I_2\\ I_3 \end{bmatrix} = \rho\,\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{n},\quad \mathbf{S}=\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{s}_1^{T}\\ \mathbf{s}_2^{T}\\ \mathbf{s}_3^{T} \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \rho\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{I},\quad \rho=\lVert \mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{I}\rVert,\quad \mathbf{n}=\frac{\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{I}}{\rho} \]

This requires \(\mathbf{S}\) to be invertible: the three lights must not lie in one plane through the surface point. Bunch them together and noise grows quickly. Pixels in shadow (\(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{s} < 0\)) break the linear model, so they show up as errors.

03

One pixel in gradient space

Click any pixel in the three images above. A surface direction can be written as the gradient \((p, q) = (\partial z/\partial x,\ \partial z/\partial y)\). One image constrains it only to a curve of directions with the same brightness. Three images give three curves that meet at one point.

The reflectance map

\[ R(p,q) = \rho\,\frac{-p\,s_x - q\,s_y + s_z}{\sqrt{1+p^2+q^2}} \]

Each curve is a contour \(R_k(p,q) = I_k\), the set of directions that would produce the brightness observed under light \(k\). Two curves can cross at two points. The third light settles which one is correct.

04

From normals to shape

The normals give the slope at each pixel. Integrating the slopes gives the height \(z(x, y)\). Here this uses the Frankot–Chellappa method, which finds the surface whose slopes best match in the least-squares sense. Drag to rotate, scroll or pinch to zoom, and choose which of the three lights shines on it: the surface is shaded with the same Lambert law as the images above.
