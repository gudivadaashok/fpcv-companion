Radiometry & Reflectance, Interactively

A pixel is bright because light arrived at a surface, the surface scattered some of it, and the camera collected what left in its direction. Here you can change each step and watch the numbers.

Follows the Radiometry and Reflectance lectures of First Principles of Computer Vision. The models are the standard textbook forms, evaluated for one white point light and a camera looking straight down the z axis.

00

How much light lands on a patch?

Drag the yellow light around the patch, or use the sliders. Two things change the irradiance \(E\): the angle from the surface normal, and the distance.

The equation

\[ E = \frac{I \cos\theta}{r^2} \]

The source has intensity \(I = 100\) W/sr here. Tilting the patch spreads the same beam over a longer footprint, \(1/\cos\theta\) times larger, so the light per unit area falls as \(\cos\theta\). Moving the source away spreads the light over a sphere of area \(4\pi r^2\), so the irradiance falls as \(1/r^2\). Try \(r = 1.5\) and \(r = 3\): doubling the distance cuts \(E\) to a quarter.

Inspect one pixel

Click the sphere above, or here. The left plot shows the BRDF in the plane that holds the light and the normal. The right panel plugs the pixel's angles into the formula.

Reading the plot

Each point on the curve is a viewing direction in the plane of incidence. Its distance from the center is \(f\). Lambertian is a perfect half-circle ring: the same in every direction. A glossy surface adds a lobe around the mirror direction, the reflection of the light about the normal. Raise roughness and the lobe widens and drops. Oren–Nayar brightens the return toward the light (backscatter), which flat-looking rough surfaces such as the Moon show.

If the viewer is out of the plane of incidence (\(\varphi\) is not \(0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\)), the exact value can differ from the curve. The ring on the curve shows only the in-plane angle \(\theta_r\).
