Shape from Shading, Interactively

One photograph, one light. Each pixel's brightness limits the surface slope there to a curve of possibilities, and a smooth, connected surface has to pick one point from every curve.

Everything is Lambertian with a known light direction and albedo 1, and the true shape is known so you can measure the error. Follows the Shape from Shading lecture of First Principles of Computer Vision.

00

Render a surface under a light

Choose a shape, or upload a photo to use as a height map (brighter pixels are higher). Drag the light in the dial. The image is I = R(p, q), where (p, q) is the surface slope at that pixel.

01

One pixel gives a curve, not a point

The plot is gradient space: every point (p, q) is one surface slope. The curves are iso-brightness contours of the reflectance map R(p, q). Click a pixel in the image: the thick curve holds every slope that would give that brightness. The true slope is one point on it.

The reflectance map

R(p, q) = (s_z − p s_x − q s_y) / √(1 + p² + q²)

For a Lambertian surface, the contours are conic sections (ellipses, parabolas or hyperbolas). Gray shading marks slopes that face away from the light, where R is 0 and the pixel is in shadow. A single image therefore gives one equation for two unknowns at every pixel. The solver needs extra assumptions: the surface is smooth, and its edges are known.

02

The bump and the crater look the same

Turn a bump into a crater by negating its height. That negates every slope, and flipping the light direction in the image plane negates the matching terms in R. The two pictures below use the same light as above, mirrored for the crater.

The images match to rounding error. Your visual system resolves this by assuming light comes from above. A solver has no such assumption, so it needs a prior or known boundary values to choose.

03

Recover the shape iteratively

This is a Horn-style variational method. It lowers an energy made of the image error plus a smoothness penalty on the surface, by momentum gradient descent, with the boundary condition Z = 0 on the image border. It starts coarse (24 × 24), then refines to 48 and 96 pixels. The page starts after 300 iterations. On the default dome the solver often returns a dent instead of a dome, which is the ambiguity from stage 02. Try the Bumps or Crater surface for a close match. Press Reset to start from a flat surface, then Run.

The energy

E(Z) = Σ [ I − R(p, q) ]² + λ Σ ‖∇Z‖², p = (Z(x+1,y) − Z(x−1,y))/2, q = (Z(x,y+1) − Z(x,y−1))/2

Z ← Z + v, v ← 0.9 v − η ∂E/∂Z, Z = 0 on the border

The first term asks the surface to reproduce the image. The second asks for smoothness, which supplies the missing equation at each pixel. A large λ flattens the result, and a small λ lets noise and the bump/crater ambiguity through. Honest limits: one light and no known interior heights make the problem under-determined, so the answer depends on the boundary and on λ. The dome is the hardest shape here: its shading is nearly the same for several surfaces, and the solver settles on one of them. Pixels in shadow (R = 0) give no data and are filled by smoothness only. Other methods, such as Tsai–Shah's linear approximation, make different trade-offs and need more care to stay stable.
