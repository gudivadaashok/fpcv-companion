Uncalibrated Stereo, Interactively

Two photographs of the same scene constrain each other. A point in one image must lie on a line in the other, even when you know nothing about the cameras. Estimate that constraint from matches, then use it, or a rectified pair, to measure depth.

Part 1 estimates the fundamental matrix with the normalized 8-point algorithm. Part 2 finds dense disparity by block matching. All data is generated in your browser, so the truth is known. Follows the Uncalibrated Stereo lectures.

Part 1 · Epipolar geometry
00

Two cameras, one scene

A cloud of 3D points sits in front of two cameras. The left camera defines the world frame. The right camera is moved and turned. Drag the view to rotate it, scroll or pinch to zoom. The algorithm below never sees the 3D points or the cameras. It sees only the two images.

The true fundamental matrix

With intrinsics \(K\), \(K'\) and a relative pose \(\mathbf{X}' = R\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{t}\), a left pixel \(\mathbf{x}\) and its right match \(\mathbf{x}'\) satisfy

\[ \mathbf{x}'^\top F\, \mathbf{x} = 0, \qquad F = K'^{-\top} [\mathbf{t}]_\times R\, K^{-1} \]

The parallel pose makes epipolar lines horizontal. That is the rectified case used in Part 2.

01

Estimate \(F\) with the 8-point algorithm

Each match gives one linear equation in the nine entries of \(F\). Add noise to the matched pixels and change how many matches you use. Then switch off normalization or the rank-2 step to see why each one matters.

0.5
40
  1. Normalize. Shift each image's points to zero mean and scale so the mean distance from the origin is \(\sqrt{2}\). This gives transforms \(T\) and \(T'\) and keeps the equations well conditioned.
  2. Stack the equations. Each match \((x, y) \leftrightarrow (x', y')\) gives the row \([x'x,\ x'y,\ x',\ y'x,\ y'y,\ y',\ x,\ y,\ 1]\). Find the eigenvector of \(A^\top A\) with the smallest eigenvalue. That is \(\hat F\), reshaped to \(3 \times 3\).
  3. Enforce rank 2. A true \(F\) is singular, so every epipolar line passes through one epipole. Noise makes the estimate full rank. Set its smallest singular value to zero: \(\hat F \leftarrow \hat F (I - \mathbf{v}_3\mathbf{v}_3^\top)\), where \(\mathbf{v}_3\) is the right singular vector for that value.
  4. Denormalize. \(F = T'^\top \hat F\, T\).
02

Epipolar lines

Click anywhere in the left image. The line \(F\mathbf{x}\) appears in the right image: estimated in color, true dashed. If you click within a few pixels of a scene point, the click snaps to it and a ring marks its true match in the right image, which should sit on both lines.

The images are point clouds drawn without hidden-surface removal, so points can show through the sphere and box. Matches are exact apart from the noise you add. Real matching also produces outliers, which this page does not simulate. A method such as RANSAC would handle them.

Part 2 · Dense matching on a rectified pair
03

A rectified pair with known disparity

When the epipolar lines are horizontal, a point at \((x, y)\) in the left image appears at \((x - d, y)\) in the right image. The disparity \(d\) is large for near surfaces and small for far ones. This pair is a texture pasted onto a known disparity map. Holes left by occlusion are filled from neighboring pixels.

2

Use your own rectified pair

Both images must be the same size and already rectified. They are shrunk to 256 × 192 and converted to gray. There is no ground truth for your pair, so the error maps stay empty.
04

Block matching

For each left pixel, slide a window along the same row of the right image. Pick the disparity with the best match score. A small window keeps detail but is noisy. A large window is stable but blurs depth edges. Click any map to choose a pixel.

Match scores for a window \(W\) around \((x, y)\)

\[ \mathrm{SSD}(d) = \sum_{W} \big[ L(x{+}i,\, y{+}j) - R(x{-}d{+}i,\, y{+}j) \big]^2 \qquad \mathrm{SAD}(d) = \sum_{W} \big| L - R \big| \] \[ \mathrm{NCC}(d) = \frac{\sum_{W} (L - \bar L)(R - \bar R)}{\sqrt{\sum_{W} (L - \bar L)^2 \cdot \sum_{W} (R - \bar R)^2}} \]

SSD and SAD choose the smallest value. NCC chooses the largest and ignores gain and offset, so it survives a brighter right image. The page refines the winner to sub-pixel precision with a parabola through the three scores around it. With \(f = 300\) px and baseline \(B = 8\) cm, the depth in cm is \(Z = 2400 / d\).

One pixel's matching cost

The curve shows the score at every disparity for the pixel you clicked. A good match is one sharp minimum. In flat or repeating texture the curve has a wide valley or several equal dips, and the window cannot decide.
